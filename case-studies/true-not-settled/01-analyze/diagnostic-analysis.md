## 2. The five items

### Q1. Role of boldface portions (correct)

> Visits to Ashford's branch libraries have fallen by a quarter since the city launched an e-lending app two years ago. **The app is very probably part of the reason**: **the branches serving neighborhoods with the most app sign-ups saw the steepest drops**. The city council is considering cutting branch hours to save money. A better plan is to keep the hours and shift branch programming toward what the app cannot offer, such as study space and after-school tutoring.
>
> In the argument, the two portions in boldface play which of the following roles?
>
> (A) The first is evidence supporting the second; the second is the argument's main conclusion.
> (B) The first is a position the argument rejects; the second is evidence against that position.
> (C) The first is the argument's main conclusion; the second is evidence supporting it.
> (D) The first is a conclusion that supports the argument's main conclusion; the second is evidence supporting the first.
> (E) The first is an intermediate conclusion; the second is the argument's main conclusion.

**Key: D.** The main point is the recommendation to keep hours and shift programming. Bold 1 (the app is part of the reason) is supported by Bold 2 and in turn supports the recommendation. A reverses the roles, B denies Bold 1 is a conclusion, and C and E make a bold portion the main point. The learner found the recommendation first and traced the support chain before reading the choices. **[Certain]**

### Q2. Evaluate, EXCEPT (correct)

> Last year Corvex Logistics replaced its single national dispatch team with four regional teams. This year, the share of deliveries arriving late fell from 12 percent to 7 percent. Management concludes that the regional structure caused the improvement, because regional dispatchers know local road conditions better.
>
> Each of the following would be useful to know in evaluating the argument EXCEPT:
>
> (A) whether this year's weather was milder than last year's
> (B) how many delivery vehicles the company owned at the start of last year
> (C) whether each regional dispatcher handles fewer routes than a national dispatcher did
> (D) whether the company also adopted new route-planning software this year
> (E) whether late deliveries fell most in the regions where local road knowledge matters most

**Key: B.** A and D are rival causes, C is an alternative mechanism (lighter workload), and E tests the stated mechanism. B is a one-time snapshot that says nothing about what changed. The learner's method: list rival causes, then test the mechanism. **[Certain]**

### Q3. Evaluate, LEAST useful (wrong: chose A, key B)

> Two years ago, Brennick's permit office began requiring applicants to complete an online checklist before submitting a building-permit application. Since then, the number of applications approved on first review has risen by 40 percent. The office concludes that the checklist has improved the quality of applications.
>
> Which of the following would be LEAST useful to know in evaluating the argument?
>
> (A) the number of applications submitted in each of the last four years
> (B) what share of applications submitted the year before the checklist came from licensed architects
> (C) whether reviewers' approval standards changed when the checklist was introduced
> (D) whether most applicants actually completed the checklist
> (E) whether the office added reviewers during the period

**Key: B.** The conclusion is about *quality*, but the evidence is a *count* of approvals. A count can rise simply because more applications arrived, so A is highly useful. B is a single pre-change share with no trend and no comparison: least useful.

*Learner's note on A:* "How many came in doesn't change that more got approved. Same as the dispatch one."

The learner reused the Q2 method. In Q2 the metric was a share, so volume didn't matter; in Q3 it is a count, so it does. **[Certain]** for the error; **[Judgement]** that the reuse caused it.

### Q4. Weaken (wrong: chose D, key B)

> A van maker's warranty records show that 4 percent of its delivery-van batteries develop cell swelling within three years. Among batteries removed after a thermal-sensor alarm, however, inspectors found swelling in 9 percent. On the basis of this comparison, the company's engineers conclude that thermal-alarm events make swelling more likely.
>
> Which of the following, if true, most seriously weakens the engineers' conclusion?
>
> (A) High temperatures are known to speed up the chemical reactions that cause swelling.
> (B) Swelling in batteries that operate normally is rarely detected unless the battery is removed and opened, which warranty servicing seldom involves.
> (C) Both groups of batteries came from the same supplier.
> (D) Inspectors used a new imaging scanner that detects smaller swelling than earlier methods.
> (E) Most of the company's vans operate in hot climates.

**Key: B.** The conclusion rests on comparing 4 percent with 9 percent. B shows the 4 percent undercounts swelling in normal batteries, which are rarely opened, so the true rate may match or exceed 9 percent and the gap disappears. D works only if you add a premise that the scanner was used on one group alone. A confirms the mechanism; C and E don't distinguish the groups.

*Learner's notes:* "Numbers look fine. Going to the options." On B: "That's about paperwork, not batteries." **[Certain]**

### Q5. Weaken (wrong: chose C, key D)

> Over the past ten years, confirmed cases of invoice fraud submitted through Halden Bank's online portal have risen fivefold, while confirmed cases at its branch counters have stayed roughly flat. A bank analyst concludes that a business customer who submits invoices online is now more likely to be defrauded than one who submits them at a branch.
>
> Which of the following, if true, most seriously weakens the analyst's conclusion?
>
> (A) Fraudsters increasingly target online channels because identities are easier to fake there.
> (B) Both channels use the same verification checks.
> (C) Many customers use both channels, and the bank records each customer under the channel they use most.
> (D) The number of invoices submitted through the portal has grown more than tenfold over the period, while branch submissions have fallen.
> (E) The bank's definition of confirmed fraud has not changed in ten years.

**Key: D.** The conclusion is about *likelihood*, but the evidence is a *count* of frauds over time. D shows portal volume grew faster than fraud, so the rate per invoice may have fallen. C is irrelevant, because customers are grouped by their main channel either way. The learner handled A (strengthens), B and E (apply equally) correctly.

*Learner's note on D:* "Still confirmed cases, so the rise is real." **[Certain]**

## 4. Strengths to protect

Identifies main and intermediate conclusions precisely; maps argument structure; attacks causal claims with rival causes and mechanism tests; rejects options that apply equally to both groups; writes a reason for every elimination. **[Certain]** for the observed items.

## 5. Root cause

**A true number is treated as a settled number.** The learner checks whether evidence is *true*, not whether it *fits* the conclusion. **[Judgement, strongly supported by the learner's own notes in Q3, Q4 and Q5]**

## 6. Two gaps and one trigger

| | Definition | Evidence | Confidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Gap 1: count vs rate** | When the conclusion is about a rate, likelihood or quality and the evidence is a raw count, the learner doesn't ask what happened to the denominator. | Q3, Q5 | [Judgement], strongly supported (2 of 2 relevant items) |
| **Gap 2: basis of comparison** | When the conclusion rests on a comparison, the learner doesn't check that both sides are comparable: which groups, grouped on which side (cause, effect or time), and measured alike. | Q4 (records vs inspection); Q5 (a comparison over time used to support a comparison between channels) | [Judgement], supported (2 items) |
| **Trigger (behaviour)** | On numeric arguments the pre-think is skipped or reused from a previous question, and the learner moves fast, so neither check runs. | Q4: "Going to the options." Q3: "Same as the dispatch one." Wrong answers at 1:35 to 1:50 vs 2:10 to 2:30 on correct ones. | [Judgement] (5 data points) |

The gaps sit in core reasoning skills, not in foundations, so the remedy is targeted and does not repeat the course. **[Judgement]**

## 7. Limits

- **Five questions, no platform history.** The diagnosis is a hypothesis about specific question types. U0 screens it; U3 confirms it on 20+ numeric items.
- **If U0 shows strong numeric performance,** the diagnosis is overstated, and the pathway routes the learner straight to U3 to check.
- **Open items:** the learner's target score (sets the exit bar) and practice history (concept gap vs deployment gap).
